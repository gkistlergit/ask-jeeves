# Restrictive covenants: current law (Federal, Indiana, South Carolina), as of 2026-10-20

Ticket: `.scratch/ask-jeeves/issues/02-restrictive-covenants-law.md`. Depth: deep. All sources accessed 2026-10-20. Not legal advice.

## Summary

1. **Federal (CHANGED 2025–26):** the FTC non-compete rule is gone. The N.D. Tex. set it aside nationwide on 2024-08-20 (*Ryan*), and the M.D. Fla. ruled against it on 2024-08-15 (*Properties of the Villages*). [Corrected 2026-10-20: was "Courts set it aside in 2024."] The FTC voted 3-1 on 2025-09-05 to drop its appeals, and it removed 16 CFR Part 910 from the CFR on 2026-02-12. The FTC now works case by case under Section 5: consent orders (Gateway, Adamas no-hire, Rollins), warning letters, an RFI and a workshop.
2. **NLRB (CHANGED 2025–26):** GC 25-05 (2025-02-14) rescinded the non-compete memos (GC 23-08, GC 25-01) and the *McLaren Macomb* guidance (GC 23-05). GC Carey (GC 26-04, 2026-08-26) is asking the Board to overrule *McLaren Macomb* and *Stericycle*. The Board still applied *McLaren* as "extant precedent" in April 2026.
3. **Indiana (CHANGED 2025–26):** SEA 475 (P.L. 207-2025) voids physician non-competes with hospitals and hospital systems for agreements first made on or after 2025-07-01. Every general or other-industry bill died in 2025 and 2026, and no 2026 enacted act touches non-competes.
4. **Indiana common law:** covenants are disfavored and need a legitimate interest plus reasonable time, place and activity. The blue pencil only erases; reformation clauses can't expand it. Continued at-will employment is enough consideration.
5. **South Carolina:** no 2025–26 change was enacted. The physician ban H. 4767 passed the House and cleared Senate committee, then stalled. Common law sets a five-part test. There is no blue pencil, and courts may not rewrite or extend a covenant. A covenant signed mid-employment needs separate consideration (*Poole*).
6. **NDAs:** SC Code § 39-8-30(D) says a trade-secret NDA needs no time or geographic limit. SC scrutinizes an NDA broad enough to act as a non-compete as one (*Milliken* n.4; *Fay*). Indiana: no authority in this file supports the same rule (UNVERIFIED; added 2026-10-20, the earlier text said "both states").

## Federal

### FTC non-compete rule: status

- **[F1] The rule is not in effect.** The FTC's rule page says the rule "is not in effect and it is not enforceable." A district court stopped enforcement on 2024-08-20, the FTC appealed on 2024-10-18, and on 2025-09-05 it moved to dismiss. — FTC rule page. Verified.
- **[F2] CHANGED 2025–26: the FTC voted to drop its appeals.** On 2025-09-05 the Commission voted 3-1 to dismiss *Ryan, LLC v. FTC*, No. 24-10951 (5th Cir.) and *Properties of the Villages v. FTC*, No. 24-13102 (11th Cir.) and to accede to vacatur. Ferguson and Holyoak voted in favor, with Meador concurring; Slaughter dissented. — FTC press release, 2025-09-05. Verified. (The release has no forward-looking enforcement plan.)
- **[F3] CHANGED 2025–26: the rule was removed from the CFR.** A final rule, FR Doc. 2026-02866, effective 2026-02-12, removes and reserves 16 CFR Part 910. It is titled "Revision of the Negative Option Rule, Withdrawal of the CARS Rule, Removal of the Non-Compete Rule To Conform These Rules to Federal Court Decisions." The FTC skipped notice and comment under the APA good-cause exception (5 U.S.C. 553(b)(B)), calling the step ministerial. — federalregister.gov XML full text. Verified. Citation **91 FR 6507** confirmed in the Federal Register API record (volume 91, start page 6507; effective 2026-02-12), and eCFR's version history shows §§ 910.1–910.4 removed on 2026-02-12. Immediate effect rests on 5 U.S.C. 553(d)(3). (Upgraded 2026-10-20.)
- **[F4] Fifth Circuit dismissal date.** The Fifth Circuit clerk dismissed No. 24-10951 under FRAP 42(b) on the FTC's motion, filed 2025-09-08 (Doc. 228-2). — Certified copy of the clerk's order, hosted by the U.S. Chamber. Verified (upgraded 2026-10-20; the court's own docket was not opened).

### Federal statutes that limit or shape NDAs and covenants

- **[F18] DTSA whistleblower immunity notice, 18 U.S.C. § 1833(b)(3).** Any employee contract governing trade secrets or confidential information must give notice of the immunity; a cross-reference to a policy document is enough. Without the notice, the employer cannot recover exemplary damages or attorney fees against that employee. This applies to contracts entered into or updated after 2016-05-11. — LII copy of statute. Verified.
- **[F19] DTSA injunction limits, 18 U.S.C. § 1836(b)(3)(A).** An injunction cannot "prevent a person from entering into an employment relationship." Conditions must rest on evidence of threatened misappropriation and cannot conflict with state law prohibiting restraints of trade. Exemplary damages can reach 2x for willful and malicious misappropriation, and fees are available. The limitations period is three years from discovery (§ 1836(d)). — LII copy. Verified.
- **[F20] Speak Out Act, 42 U.S.C. § 19403.** Pre-dispute NDAs and non-disparagement clauses are not judicially enforceable in sexual assault or harassment disputes. § 19403(d) preserves protection of trade secrets and proprietary information. — LII copy. Verified.
- **[F21] SEC Rule 21F-17(a), 17 CFR 240.21F-17.** No person may impede direct communication with SEC staff about possible securities violations, including by enforcing a confidentiality agreement. — LII copy. Verified.
- **[F22] No federal statute bans non-competes.** I found no enacted one. (This is a negative finding.)
- **[F23]** The status of the January 2025 FTC/DOJ Antitrust Guidelines for Business Activities Affecting Workers (adopted 3-2 over Ferguson and Holyoak's dissent) is UNVERIFIED. I found no withdrawal notice, and secondary sources could not confirm their current status.

## Indiana

### Statutes

- **[I1] Physician non-compete chapter, IC 25-22.5-5.5.** It applies to physician non-competes originally entered into on or after 2020-07-01 (§ 1). — 2026 IC (iga.in.gov). Verified.
  - **§ 2(a) (2020):** to be enforceable, the agreement must include five terms: notice copies, patient contact information, record access, a buyout option at a reasonable price, and records format. — SEA 7 (2023) text and 2026 IC. Verified.
  - **§ 2(b) (2023):** beginning 2023-07-01, the agreement is unenforceable if the employer fires the physician without cause, the physician quits for cause, or the contract expired with both sides' obligations met. — SEA 7, pg. 2. Verified.
  - **§ 2.5 (2023):** a primary care physician (family, general pediatric, internal medicine) and an employer may not enter a non-compete. This applies to agreements originally entered into on or after 2023-07-01. — SEA 7, pg. 1, 3. Verified. Public Law 165, signed 2023-05-04 (IGA API). Verified.
  - **§ 2.6 (2023):** the buyout price is negotiated in good faith. If they can't agree on a price, either side may serve notice of mediation within 35 days of the physician's buyout election. Mediation must end within 45 days of that notice. — SEA 7, pg. 3. Verified.
- **[I2] CHANGED 2025–26: SEA 475 (2025), P.L. 207-2025, signed 2025-05-06, effective 2025-07-01.** — IGA bill page and enrolled text. Verified.
  - **New § 2.3:** a physician and a hospital, a hospital's parent company, an affiliated manager or a hospital system may not enter a non-compete on or after 2025-07-01. Violating agreements are "void and unenforceable" (§ 2.3(c)). — SEA 475, pg. 3.
  - **Broad definition (§ 1.4(a)):** a non-compete includes any provision that bars practice with a new employer, requires employer consent, or requires the physician to submit to equitable relief. It also includes indirect restrictions. Stay-or-pay terms are covered: repayment of bonuses or training costs for a physician employed at least 3 years, when triggered by leaving to practice elsewhere. — SEA 475, pg. 2.
  - **Exclusions (§ 1.4(b)):** NDAs protecting confidential information or trade secrets; employee non-solicits of up to 1 year that don't restrict patient interactions, referrals, clinical collaboration or professional relationships; and sale-of-business covenants where the physician owns more than 50%. — SEA 475, pg. 2–3.
  - **Grandfathering (§ 1.6):** "originally entered into" means the first signing. Amendments and renewals don't reset the date, so pre-2025-07-01 agreements keep their old status even if renewed. — SEA 475, pg. 3.
  - **§ 1.7(b):** purely executive or managerial work without direct patient care is not "practice of medicine." — SEA 475, pg. 3.
  - **Not covered:** non-hospital employers of specialists. PAs, APRNs and nurses are outside the chapter (it addresses physicians only). Verified from the text's scope.
- **[I3] No 2026 change to the chapter.** The 2026 IC history lines for every section of IC 25-22.5-5.5 show only P.L. 93-2020, P.L. 165-2023 and P.L. 207-2025. — 2026 IC. Verified.
- **[I4] CHANGED 2025–26 (negative): failed bills.** HB 1625 (2025, general ban), SB 132 (2026, ban below $150,000 a year; referred to Senate Judiciary 2026-01-05) and HB 1054 (2026, plumbing trade ban) never moved past their introduced version. — IGA API bill details. Verified.
- **[I5] No other 2025 or 2026 enacted act addresses non-competes.** I scanned the digests of all 246 enrolled 2025 bills and all 164 enrolled 2026 bills. Only SEA 475 mentions non-competes. — IGA API, my scan. Verified (digest-level; a provision buried in a bill's body could be missed).
- **[I6] No general non-compete statute in the 2026 Indiana Code.** A full-text scan of all 2026 IC titles for "noncompet" or "covenant not to compete" found only IC 25-22.5-5.5, IC 23-2-2.7-1(9) and IC 2-5-47-7 (a 2023 task-force study item, P.L. 203-2023). Under IC 23-2-2.7-1(9), a franchise covenant longer than 3 years or wider than the exclusive area is unlawful. IC 27-16-3-2 says a professional employer agreement doesn't modify a client's restrictive covenants. — 2026 IC. Verified.
- **[I7] Indiana Uniform Trade Secrets Act, IC 24-2-3.** It displaces conflicting law "except contract law and criminal law" (§ 1(c)), so NDA contract claims survive alongside it. Actual or threatened misappropriation may be enjoined (§ 3). Exemplary damages reach 2x for willful and malicious misappropriation (§ 4(c)). Fees are available for bad faith or willful and malicious conduct (§ 5). The limitations period is 3 years from discovery (§ 7). — 2025 IC. Verified.

### Reasonableness test (common law)

- **[I8]** Covenants are "in restraint of trade and disfavored by the law" and strictly construed against employers. The employer must first show a legitimate protectable interest. — *Heraeus Medical, LLC v. Zimmer, Inc.*, No. 19S-PL-471 (Ind. Dec. 3, 2019) (FindLaw copy). Verified.
- **[I9]** The employer must then show the restraint is reasonable "as to the time, activity, and geographic area restricted." Reasonableness is a question of law, and the employer bears the burden. Protectable goodwill includes the "advantageous familiarity and personal contact" employees gain with customers. Physician covenants get "particularly careful scrutiny" but are not per se void. — *Central Indiana Podiatry, P.C. v. Krueger*, No. 29S05-0706-CV-256 (Ind. Mar. 11, 2008) (FindLaw copy). Verified.
- **[I10]** Geography must track where the employee actually worked. *Krueger* enforced the covenant only in the three counties where the podiatrist practiced in his last two years. — *Krueger*. Verified.
- **[I11]** Activity restrictions that reach work the employee never did are overbroad. Examples: a ban on working for a competitor "in any capacity" (*Med-1 Solutions, LLC v. Taylor*, No. 24A-PL-450 (Ind. Ct. App. Nov. 25, 2024)), and a covenant covering every customer and all competitive services (*Clark's Sales & Service, Inc. v. Smith*, No. 49A02-1306-PL-552 (Ind. Ct. App. Feb. 20, 2014)). General skills and knowledge are not protectable (*Buffkin v. Glacier Group*, No. 79A02-1302-PL-141 (Ind. Ct. App. Oct. 7, 2013)). — FindLaw copies. Verified. **Correction to prior research:** *Buffkin* did not separately analyze the 3-year term. It struck the covenant on geography (continental US) and activity scope.

### Blue pencil versus reformation

- **[I12]** Indiana's blue pencil is "really an eraser." Courts may strike unreasonable, divisible language but may not add, change or rearrange terms. A reformation clause doesn't let a court go further. The doctrine applies to all restrictive covenants in the agreement. — *Heraeus* (FindLaw copy). Verified.
- **[I13]** If striking words can't leave a reasonable restriction, the whole covenant fails. *Clark's Sales* called its covenant "an indiscrete whole," and *Med-1* held a severability clause asking the court to reduce the restraint "inoperative." — FindLaw copies. Verified.
- **[I14]** Whether a tiered step-down clause works in Indiana is unknown. I found no Indiana appellate decision on one (prior-research lead, not re-verified by search because the search budget ran out). See Open questions.

### Consideration (including mid-employment)

- **[I15]** An employer's promise to continue at-will employment is valid consideration, including for a covenant signed years into employment under "sign or be fired." *Med-1* quotes *Ackerman*: "An employer's promise to continue at-will employment is valid consideration." — *Med-1* (FindLaw copy). Verified. *Ackerman v. Kimball Int'l* was not opened; it is verified only as quoted in *Med-1*.

### Non-solicits and NDAs

- **[I16]** An employee non-solicit covering "any individual employed" by the company is overbroad unless limited to employees whose knowledge would give a competitor an unfair advantage. If no deletion cures it, it is void. — *Heraeus*. Verified.
- **[I17]** A customer non-solicit is a restrictive covenant subject to the same blue pencil (*Heraeus*, citing a case that blue-penciled a customer non-solicit). — Verified. Indiana's treatment of "prospective customer" clauses comes only from a secondary source (Ogletree 2020, on the Court of Appeals' handling in *Heraeus*). UNVERIFIED.
- **[I18]** (Narrowed 2026-10-20: the earlier text said NDA breach "requires proof of misuse.") On a preliminary-injunction record, *Med-1* required evidence that the employee took or threatened to take information. Forensic logs showing file access, without proof of taking or use, fell short. The court recognized inevitable disclosure only "on extreme facts," calling the employer's claim "little more than speculation." — *Med-1*. Verified.

### Industry-specific rules

- Physicians: see [I1]–[I3]. Franchises: see [I6]. Nothing verified for other professions in 2025–26.

### Tolling

- **[I19]** No verified Indiana authority on contractual tolling clauses or on court-ordered extension of a restricted period was found. *Krueger* notes the period ran out during the appeal but decided the issue anyway, and no tolling was discussed. *Clark's Sales* and *Buffkin* did not reach the tolling clauses before them. — Verified as negatives. UNVERIFIED as to the actual rule.

### Remedies

- **[I20]** Preliminary injunction test: reasonable likelihood of success, inadequate legal remedy (irreparable harm), balance of harms, and no disservice to the public interest. Lost patients and referrals that are hard to quantify can show an inadequate legal remedy. — *Krueger*; *Med-1*. Verified. For trade secrets, see the IUTSA [I7].

## South Carolina

- **[S4] SC Trade Secrets Act, S.C. Code § 39-8-30.**

### Reasonableness test

- **[S5] Five-part test.** A covenant is upheld only if it is (1) necessary to protect the employer's legitimate interest, (2) reasonably limited in time and place, (3) not unduly harsh on the employee's livelihood, (4) reasonable under public policy, and (5) supported by valuable consideration. Covenants are disfavored and strictly construed against the employer. A geographic limit is generally reasonable if confined to territory where the employee had customer contact. — *Rental Uniform Serv. of Florence, Inc. v. Dudley*, 278 S.C. 674, 301 S.E.2d 142 (1983), as quoted in *Team IA, Inc. v. Lucas*, Op. No. 4889 (S.C. Ct. App. 2011, refiled Oct. 20, 2011), 395 S.C. 237, 717 S.E.2d 103, and *Fay v. Total Quality Logistics, LLC*, Op. No. 5471 (S.C. Ct. App. Mar. 1, 2017), pg. 7. Verified via the quoting opinions. The *Dudley* original was not opened; its citation was confirmed through CourtListener's citation resolver.
- **[S6]** A covenant with no geographic limit is void, and a court cannot add one. — *Stonhard, Inc. v. Carolina Flooring Specialists, Inc.*, Op. No. 26047 (S.C. Oct. 10, 2005), 366 S.C. 156, 621 S.E.2d 352. Verified.
- **[S7]** Time limits of 2 to 3 years have been approved in the employment cases cited (*Dudley* 3 years; *Delmar Studios* 2 years). — as stated in *Fay*, pg. 7. Verified via *Fay*.
- **[S8] Sale-of-business covenants get "a more relaxed level" of scrutiny than employment covenants.** *Palmetto Mortuary* upheld a 150-mile restriction in an asset purchase agreement. — *Palmetto Mortuary Transport, Inc. v. Knight Systems, Inc.*, Op. No. 27833 (S.C. Aug. 29, 2018), reversing 416 S.C. 427, 786 S.E.2d 588 (Ct. App. 2016). Verified.

### Blue pencil versus reformation

- **[S9] No blue pencil.** Restrictions "must stand or fall on their own terms." A trial court that replaced a 75-mile radius with its own 15-mile radius was reversed. — *Poynter Investments, Inc. v. Century Builders of Piedmont, Inc.*, Op. No. 26821 (S.C. May 24, 2010), 387 S.C. 583, 694 S.E.2d 15. Verified. *Fay* repeats that SC "does not follow the 'blue pencil' rule" and refused to import a 1-year limit from another paragraph (pg. 6, 9). Verified.
- **[S10] Step-down clauses (Court of Appeals).** In *Team IA*, a nationwide restriction was overbroad, but the agreement's alternative four-state territory "would remain valid and enforceable" if the facts support it. The court remanded for fact-finding. — *Team IA*. Verified. In *Palmetto Mortuary*, the Court of Appeals also noted the covenant lacked a "step-down provision" (as recounted in Op. 27833). **Tension:** the *Poynter* agreement had a three-tier step-down, but the Supreme Court decided only that the trial judge couldn't write his own term. It did not rule on the tiers. See Open questions.
- **[S11] Choice of law.** SC honors choice-of-law clauses but won't enforce a foreign-law reformation that violates SC public policy, such as adding a term the parties never agreed to. — *Stonhard*; *Team IA*; *Fay* (majority and Geathers concurrence differ on whether the foreign state's modification rules apply first). Verified. **Conflict noted:** the *Fay* majority and concurrence split on method.

### Consideration (including mid-employment)

- **[S12]** A covenant signed at the start of at-will employment is supported by the employment itself (*Riedman Corp. v. Jarosh*, as discussed in *Poole*). A covenant signed after employment began requires "separate consideration, in addition to continued at-will employment." In *Poole*, the employee's duties, position and salary were unchanged, so the covenant failed. A raise, promotion or change in duties sufficed in *Standard Register v. Kerrigan* (as discussed). — *Poole v. Incentives Unlimited, Inc.*, Op. No. 25299 (S.C. June 4, 2001), 345 S.C. 378, 548 S.E.2d 207. Verified.

### Non-solicits and NDAs

- **[S13]** Customer non-solicits are analyzed as covenants under the same test. *Team IA* tied territory to customer contact, citing *Standard Register*'s enforcement of a ban on "selling to the accounts or in the territory" the employee had covered. — *Team IA*. Verified. I found no verified SC appellate holding specific to employee non-solicit or no-hire clauses (UNVERIFIED; see Open questions).
- **[S14] NDAs.** Confidentiality and invention-assignment clauses are not in restraint of trade, carry no "ancient disfavor," and are not strictly construed against the employer. They are still reviewed for reasonableness: no greater than necessary and not unduly harsh. A confidentiality clause without a time limit is not automatically invalid, but a holdover invention clause must be reasonably limited in time. Footnote 4: NDAs "so broad as to effectively become non-compete agreements" face the higher burden. — *Milliken & Co. v. Morin*, Op. No. 27154 (S.C. Aug. 1, 2012), 399 S.C. 23, 731 S.E.2d 288. Verified.
- **[S15]** An NDA that defined nearly all information as confidential, applied "at all times," and declared that a similar job would "inevitably" cause disclosure operated as a non-compete without a time limit. It was void, and § 39-8-30(D) did not save it. — *Fay*, pg. 6–9. Verified. (*Fay* cites *Milliken* as "339 S.C." at pg. 8 and "399 S.C." at pg. 6. The correct volume is 399; pg. 8's "339" is a typo in the opinion.)
- **[S16] No covenant needed for a trade-secret injunction.** The Court of Appeals affirmed a preliminary injunction against a former at-will employee with no restrictive covenants. It barred him from using personnel and bidding documents to bid or to solicit the employer's employees, under trade-secret and confidential-information theories. — *Jennings-Dill, Inc. v. Israel*, Op. No. 6046 (S.C. Ct. App. Jan. 24, 2024). Verified.

### Industry-specific rules

- No enacted SC industry-specific non-compete statute was found [S1]. Physician bills are pending or stalled [S2]–[S3].

### Tolling

- **[S17] No court-ordered extension.** SC courts will not extend a covenant's term past its stated expiration as a remedy; doing so "would be arbitrary." — *Stonhard*, certified question III. Verified. *Poynter* adds that the Court of Appeals in *MailSource* (356 S.C. 363 (Ct. App. 2003)) refused extension as rewriting the contract. Verified via *Poynter*. Whether a **contractual** tolling clause is enforceable was not decided in any opinion I opened (UNVERIFIED).

### Remedies

- **[S18] Preliminary injunction test.** The movant must show irreparable harm, likelihood of success and no adequate remedy at law. There is no separate "balancing the equities" step. — *Poynter*. Verified. Courts in *Stonhard* said they had no SC case awarding relief other than an injunction for breach of a covenant, and would not award damages under a void covenant. — *Stonhard*, question II. Verified. **Updated 2026-10-20:** in *Palmetto Mortuary* (2018), a sale-of-business case, the Supreme Court reinstated a special referee's order. That order granted a permanent injunction, attorneys' fees and $373,264.54 in lost-profit damages for wrongful competition. So money damages for breach of a valid covenant are available, at least in the sale-of-business context. *Stonhard*'s point is narrower: there are no damages under a covenant that was void when breached. — Op. 27833 text. Verified.
- **[S19] Recent decisions.** Official SC published-opinion keyword searches turned up no SC appellate decision from 2025–26 on employment covenants. — sccourts.org search. Verified as a negative; the search index's currency is not guaranteed.

## Facts that decide outcomes

### Employer side (what to gather)
- **When and why it was signed.** Signed at hire, or mid-employment? In SC, what new consideration came with a mid-employment signing (raise, promotion, bonus, new duties) (*Poole*)? In Indiana, continued employment is enough (*Med-1*).
- **The employee's actual footprint:** counties or territory worked, customers personally handled, and the confidential information actually accessed (*Krueger*, *Team IA*).
- **The exact covenant text.** Is each restriction divisible so a court can strike it, Indiana-style? In SC, does it have a time limit, a geographic limit and alternative tiers? Is there a choice-of-law clause?
- **Physician deals in Indiana:** the date the agreement was "originally entered into," whether the employer is a hospital or hospital affiliate, primary care status, and whether the five § 2(a) terms are present.
- **NDA scope.** Is "confidential information" limited, or does it cover everything? Is there an inevitable-disclosure clause (*Fay*)? Was the DTSA immunity notice given (18 U.S.C. § 1833(b))?
- **Evidence of taking or use:** forensic proof of downloads and use, not just file access (*Med-1*, *Jennings-Dill*).
- **Federal exposure:** whether non-competes go to low-wage or rank-and-file workers broadly. That is the FTC's enforcement pattern (Gateway, Rollins), and healthcare and staffing got warning letters.

### Employee side (what to gather)
- **Mid-employment signing in SC with nothing new in return.** The covenant likely fails (*Poole*).
- **Overbreadth:** "any capacity," all customers, nationwide, or no time limit. Indiana won't add terms and SC won't rewrite.
- **How employment ended.** For Indiana physicians, a firing without cause or a quit for cause makes the covenant unenforceable (§ 2(b)).
- **Industry and role:** physician, primary care, or hospital employer (Indiana); purely executive or managerial work (§ 1.7(b)).
- **Severance terms:** confidentiality or non-disparagement clauses are unlawful for NLRA-covered non-supervisors under *McLaren Macomb* while it stands.
- **Whether the employer can show a protectable interest** beyond general skill (*Buffkin*).

## Creative or non-obvious angles (each labeled creative)

1. **Creative: in SC, draft separate, self-contained tiers rather than relying on a court.** *Team IA* treated an alternative territory as potentially enforceable. Risk: the SC Supreme Court has not squarely approved step-downs, and *Poynter* stresses "stand or fall on their own terms." Enforceability: moderate, fact-dependent.
2. **Creative: in SC, pair a mid-employment covenant with real, documented new consideration** (a raise, a bonus tied to signing, a promotion) to clear *Poole*. Risk: nominal consideration may be challenged; the amount needed is unsettled. Enforceability: good if the new benefit is real.
3. **Creative: rely on trade-secret law instead of a non-compete.** Use SC § 39-8-30(B) and (D), the Indiana UTSA and the DTSA. *Jennings-Dill* shows an injunction is possible with no covenant at all. Risk: you must prove secrecy and misuse, and the DTSA bars injunctions that block employment. Enforceability: good with forensic proof.
4. **Creative: pay for the restraint.** Paid garden leave or a paid restricted period answers "unduly harsh" (SC prong 3) and supplies SC "separate consideration." Risk: no IN or SC case on garden leave was found, so it is untested. In-term restrictions are also less likely to be FTC targets, but that is UNVERIFIED.
5. **Creative (employee, Indiana physician): use the statutory buyout and mediation** (§ 2.6) as negotiating leverage. Also argue that hospital stay-or-pay repayments are void non-competes under § 1.4(a)(2) after 3 years. Risk: this applies only to the agreement dates and employers the statute covers.
6. **Creative (employee, either state): attack an overbroad NDA as a disguised non-compete** (*Fay*; *Milliken* n.4). Risk: fails if the NDA is narrowly defined.
7. **Creative (employer): favor narrow customer non-solicits over non-competes.** The Gateway order lets Gateway keep customer non-solicits covering customers the employee dealt with in their last 12 months. (Corrected 2026-10-20: 12 months is the look-back window, not the non-solicit's length.) The Rollins order keeps non-solicits too, but bars restricting general advertising or answering customer-initiated inquiries. Both signal where federal tolerance sits. Risk: state law still applies, and this is an inference from two consent orders.
