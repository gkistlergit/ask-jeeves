# Choice of law, forum, and a 50-state non-compete survey

Ticket: `.scratch/ask-jeeves/issues/04-choice-of-law-and-state-survey.md`
Current as of: 2026-10-20. Every source below was accessed 2026-10-20 unless a row says otherwise.
Depth: deep. This is research for designing the skill. It is not legal advice.

Markers used here:
- **CHANGED 2025–26**: enacted, amended, or taking effect in 2025 or 2026.
- **UNVERIFIED**: not confirmed against a source I opened.
- **secondary**: confirmed only through a law-firm, bar, or news summary.
- Page references are slip-opinion pages ("slip pg. N") unless a reporter page is given.

## Bottom line

1. **A choice-of-law clause is evidence, not the answer.** Indiana, South Carolina, and Delaware courts usually honor one. All three refuse it for a non-compete when the chosen law would enforce something the employee's home state treats as void, or would let a court rewrite a covenant the home state won't rewrite. See Part A2, A3 and A6.
2. **Statutes now override clauses in a growing list of states.** The employee-protective ones are CA, CO, LA, MA, MN and WA. Partial or occupation-specific ones are NM, UT and VT. North Carolina and South Carolina have general forum statutes. Florida's 2025 CHOICE Act goes the other way: it applies Florida's pro-enforcement law to Florida-based workers whatever the clause says. See Part A5.
3. **The 2025–26 wave is real.** Changes landed in at least 25 jurisdictions (Part B and the change list). The biggest:
   - Washington's near-total ban, effective 2027-06-30.
   - Wyoming's general ban (2025).
   - Tennessee's new $70,000 floor (2026).
   - Florida's CHOICE Act (2025).
   - Virginia's two amendments.
   - Healthcare bans in about a dozen states.
   - The FTC rule, now formally removed from the CFR (2026-02-12).
4. **Remote work is the main source of uncertainty.** Each statute uses its own location test (reside AND work, reside OR work, "Washington-based", time share), and the § 188 contacts split when work is spread across states. See Part A7.

## Part A: Choice of law and forum

### A1. Restatement (Second) of Conflict of Laws §§ 187, 188, 196

**§ 187 (law chosen by the parties).** Verified through *Dearborn v. Everett J. Prescott, Inc.*, 486 F. Supp. 2d 802 (S.D. Ind. 2007). It quotes § 187(1)–(3) in full at slip pg. 19. A chosen state's law applies unless:
- (2)(a) the chosen state has "no substantial relationship to the parties or the transaction" and there is no other reasonable basis for the choice; or
- (2)(b) applying it would be contrary to a fundamental policy of a state with a "materially greater interest", whose law would apply "under the rule of § 188" if there were no choice.

Comment g (quoted in *Dearborn* at slip pg. 19–20) says a fundamental policy may be one that protects a party against "superior bargaining power." Employment covenants are the standard example.
Source: https://www.govinfo.gov/content/pkg/USCOURTS-insd-1_07-cv-00078/pdf/USCOURTS-insd-1_07-cv-00078-0.pdf

**§ 188 (law governing without an effective choice).** Verified through the full quotation in *Aprio Advisory Grp., LLC v. Alexeev*, C.A. No. 2026-0508-JTL (Del. Ch. Oct. 16, 2026), slip pg. 12–13. The source is the opinion text extracted from the court's PDF.
- § 188(1): the state with "the most significant relationship" under the § 6 principles governs.
- § 188(2) contacts:
  - (a) place of contracting
  - (b) place of negotiation
  - (c) place of performance
  - (d) location of the subject matter
  - (e) domicil, residence, nationality, place of incorporation and place of business of the parties
- *Hightower Holding, LLC v. Gibson*, C.A. 2022-0086-LWW (Del. Ch. Feb. 9, 2023), slip pg. 13 nn.52 and 55 (the § 188(2) contacts are quoted in n.49 at slip pg. 12), quotes § 188 cmt. e:
  - the state of performance "has an obvious interest" in whether performance would be illegal;
  - but place of performance bears "little weight" when performance is divided "more or less equally" among states.
  - That second point is the remote-worker problem.
- Sources: https://courts.delaware.gov/Opinions/Download.aspx?id=402240 ; https://courts.delaware.gov/Opinions/Download.aspx?id=343830

**§ 196 (contracts for the rendition of services).** UNVERIFIED. No opinion we opened quotes its text. The ALI text is paywalled. The usual reading is that, absent an effective choice, the law of the state where the contract requires the services to be rendered (wholly or mainly) governs, unless another state has a more significant relationship. This reading is not confirmed here.
- For the skill, the verified § 188(2)(c) place-of-performance contact does the same practical work: where the employee works points to the default state.

### A2. Indiana

1. **The general rule favors the parties' choice.** *Allen v. Great Am. Reserve Ins. Co.*, 766 N.E.2d 1157, 1162 (Ind. 2002): Indiana doctrine "favors contractual stipulations as to governing law."
   - The clause governed the contract claim only. The tort and indemnity claims went to lex loci delicti (South Carolina law there).
   - Source: https://caselaw.findlaw.com/court/in-supreme-court/1153073.html. The pg. 1162 pin comes from *Dearborn* and the Indiana Commercial Court Treatise.
2. **With no clause, Indiana uses the "most intimate contacts" test** and lists the § 188(2) contacts. *Eby v. York-Division, Borg-Warner*, 455 N.E.2d 623 (Ind. Ct. App. 1983).
   - Source: https://case-law.vlex.com/vid/eby-v-york-division-887233119 (no pin cite visible). [Verifier 2026-10-20: confirmed at https://static.case.law/ne2d/455/cases/0623-01.json (Ind. Ct. App. Nov. 7, 1983). It lists the five contacts but does not label them "§ 188(2)".]
   - The Indiana Commercial Court Treatise (2025 ed., pg. 28) cites *Hubbard Mfg. v. Greeson*, 515 N.E.2d 1071, 1073 (Ind. 1987), only for the point that Indiana's own choice-of-law rules control in Indiana suits. [Verifier correction: the pin is pg. 28, not 28–29. *Hubbard* (opened at https://static.case.law/ne2d/515/cases/1071-01.json) is a wrongful-death tort case applying lex loci delicti. Don't cite it for the contract test or for honoring clauses.]
   - Source: https://www.in.gov/courts/iocs/files/commercial-court-treatise.pdf
3. **Indiana refuses an out-of-state clause for an overbroad non-compete.** *Dearborn* (S.D. Ind. 2007), slip pg. 17–32:
   - The employer chose Maine law, which would enforce the covenant to a narrowed extent. Indiana courts will not rewrite an overbroad covenant.
   - Predicting Indiana law, the court refused the Maine clause under § 187(2)(b). It applied the *Trimble v. Ameritech* public-policy factors (700 N.E.2d 1128 (Ind. 1998)) and IC 24-1-2-1, and found Indiana's interest materially greater (slip pg. 30).
   - At slip pg. 17 the court notes Indiana enforces such clauses where bargaining power is comparable, "(unlike this case)."
   - Two earlier cases, verified only as quoted in *Dearborn*:
     - *South Bend Consumers Club v. United Consumers Club*, 572 F. Supp. 209 (N.D. Ind. 1983), refused an Illinois clause in a franchise non-compete (slip pg. 20).
     - *Wright-Moore Corp. v. Ricoh Corp.*, 908 F.2d 128 (7th Cir. 1990), refused a New York clause (cited slip pg. 18, quoted slip pg. 21). [Verifier correction: these pins were given as "slip pg. 20–21".]
   - [Verifier: the reporter cite 486 F. Supp. 2d 802 (S.D. Ind. Apr. 30, 2007) is confirmed in CAP. The *Trimble* factors are at slip pg. 22–23. The PI was denied. "Superior bargaining power" falls on slip pg. 20.]
   - No Indiana state appellate case refusing a non-compete choice-of-law clause was found.
4. **Indiana courts won't rewrite covenants**, which is why the choice of law matters.
   - *Heraeus Med., LLC v. Zimmer, Inc.* (Ind. Dec. 3, 2019): the blue pencil "is really an eraser." Courts may strike divisible terms but may not add terms.
   - *Central Ind. Podiatry v. Krueger*, 882 N.E.2d 723 (Ind. 2008): physician non-competes are not per se void.
   - Sources: https://caselaw.findlaw.com/court/in-supreme-court/2034943.html ; https://caseclips.courts.in.gov/2019/12/09/heraeus-v-zimmer ; https://static.case.law/ne2d/882/cases/0723-01.json
   - The reporter cite for *Heraeus* (135 N.E.3d 150) is secondary.
5. **Forum-selection clauses are enforced if freely negotiated and reasonable and just.** The resisting party faces a "steep climb." Secondary, from the Indiana Commercial Court Treatise (pg. 117–118):
   - It summarizes *Perdue Farms, Inc. v. L&B Transp., LLC* (Ind. 2024) and *O'Bryant v. Adams*, 123 N.E.3d 689 (Ind. 2019).
   - [Verifier 2026-10-20: both opinions opened, so this point is now primary.]
     - *Perdue Farms* (Ind. Aug. 13, 2024), No. 24S-PL-40, slip pg. 5: "faces a steep climb". The clause bound the contracting party but not its non-party employees. The reporter cite 239 N.E.3d 842 is secondary.
     - *O'Bryant* (Ind. June 4, 2019), No. 18S-PL-584: a valid forum clause does not defeat personal jurisdiction. https://caselaw.findlaw.com/court/in-supreme-court/2002726.html
   - *Adsit Co. v. Gustin*, 874 N.E.2d 1018 (Ind. Ct. App. 2007), enforced a clickwrap forum clause as "reasonable and just". [Verifier: was UNVERIFIED; now confirmed at https://static.case.law/ne2d/874/cases/1018-01.json. It is a Court of Appeals case.]
6. **Indiana statutes.** Verified in the official Indiana Code 2025, https://iga.in.gov/ic/2025/Title_25.pdf.
   - IC 25-22.5-5.5 (physicians) has **no** choice-of-law or anti-waiver clause.
   - **CHANGED 2025–26:** SEA 475 (2025) added § 2.3: physicians and hospitals or hospital systems "may not enter into a noncompete agreement on or after July 1, 2025." Violations are void.
     - [Verifier correction: § 2.3 also covers a hospital's parent company and an affiliated manager.]
     - Agreements first entered into before July 1, 2025 are exempt.
     - The "P.L. 207-2025" number is UNVERIFIED: no source found. Signed 2025-05-06 per secondary sources.
     - Confirmed from the SEA 475 enrolled text (Jackson Lewis copy) and FindLaw (current to 2026-01-01). The verifier could not render iga.in.gov text.
   - § 2.5 (2023; that date is secondary) bars primary-care physician non-competes. "Notwithstanding any other law" opens § 2.5(b), and § 2.3(b) too. [Verifier correction: it does not open the section.] This supports a § 187(2)(b) fundamental-policy argument (my inference).
   - The Indiana 2026 session: secondary only. Nothing was enacted per policyrisk.com: SB 132 stalled in committee after first reading, HB 1054 (plumbers) stayed in committee. iga.in.gov did not render to confirm.

### A3. South Carolina

1. **South Carolina honors the chosen law but tests the result against SC public policy.** *Stonhard, Inc. v. Carolina Flooring Specialists, Inc.*, 366 S.C. 156, 621 S.E.2d 352 (2005), answering a certified question; the clause chose New Jersey law.
   - Non-compete terms "may be construed according to the law of another state," but the court won't enforce a result contrary to SC public policy.
   - Adding a geographic limit the parties never negotiated violates that policy.
   - Source: https://www.sccourts.org/media/opinions/HTMLFiles/SC/26047.htm
2. ***Fay v. Total Quality Logistics, LLC*, 419 S.C. 622, 799 S.E.2d 318 (Ct. App. 2017)**, Op. No. 5471, Advance Sheet No. 9:
   - An Ohio choice-of-law clause did not save an NDA that worked as a perpetual non-compete. The majority said it "must also assess" SC public policy (adv. sheet pg. 23).
   - Reformation allowed under Ohio law is still unenforceable in SC. [Verifier note: the majority did not decide Ohio law. Its n.2 (pg. 25–26) calls an Ohio analysis unnecessary, because an added term would fail in SC even if Ohio law allowed it.]
   - The concurrence (Geathers, J., pg. 27–31) quotes:
     - *Team IA, Inc. v. Lucas*, 395 S.C. 237, 248 (Ct. App. 2011): choice-of-law clauses "are generally honored in South Carolina." (pg. 28)
     - *Nucor Corp. v. Bell*, 482 F. Supp. 2d 714, 728 (D.S.C. 2007): a clause violating SC policy will not be enforced. It is quoted through *Team IA* at pg. 29.
     - Both verified only as quoted in *Fay*. The concurrence would apply Ohio law first, but finds the NDA unenforceable under Ohio law too (pg. 31).
   - Source: https://www.sccourts.org/media/opinions/advSheets/no92017.pdf
3. **SC courts do not blue-pencil.** *Poynter Invs. v. Century Builders of Piedmont*, 387 S.C. 583, 694 S.E.2d 15 (2010).
   - Quoted in *Fay*, adv. sheet pg. 28, and in https://sclawreview.org/article/sharpening-south-carolinas-blue-pencil-an-argument-for-codifying-a-strict-interpretation-of-the-blue-pencil-doctrine/.
   - [Verifier 2026-10-20: the opinion was opened at https://static.case.law/sc/387/cases/0583-01.json (S.C. May 24, 2010). Restrictions "cannot be rewritten by a court". Blue-penciling the territory was error. The agreement was tied to a business sale. Now primary.]
4. **S.C. Code § 15-7-120 (forum).** Verified: https://www.scstatehouse.gov/code/t15c007.php
   - (A): notwithstanding a clause requiring suit elsewhere, an action "alternatively may be brought" in South Carolina.
   - (B): an out-of-state arbitration-location clause is unenforceable for claims otherwise triable in SC.
   - It applies to contracts generally, not only employment. It does not void the clause; it adds an alternative SC venue.
   - Federal courts may still transfer under § 1404(a): *T.R. Helicopters, LLC v. Bell Helicopter Textron, Inc.*, C/A No. 3:10-2250-JFA (D.S.C. Nov. 17, 2010).
     - It discussed § 15-7-120, found no strong SC policy against forum clauses, and transferred to N.D. Tex.
     - [Verifier: was UNVERIFIED. The opinion text was read on vLex (https://case-law.vlex.com/vid/t-r-helicopters-llc-888806845), a copy and not the court's own site, so treat it as secondary-copy.]
5. **S.C. Code § 36-1-301 (UCC "reasonable relation")** is limited to UCC transactions, so it does not govern employment covenants (my reading of § 36-1-102). Source: https://www.scstatehouse.gov/code/t36c001.php
6. **The SC default without a clause** is traditionally lex loci contractus. [Verifier 2026-10-20: was UNVERIFIED.]
   - *Lister v. NationsBank of Del.*, 329 S.C. 133, 494 S.E.2d 449 (Ct. App. 1997), https://static.case.law/sc/329/cases/0133-01.json. The law of the place where the contract is made governs validity and interpretation. The place of performance may govern performance.
   - It applied the most-significant-relationship test only "assumptively" and did not adopt it. Treat the SC default as unsettled for remote employment.
7. **2025–26:** H.4767 (a physician non-compete ban) passed the House 58–53 on 2026-03-26. It was not enacted.
   - The Senate committee reported it favorably on 2026-05-05; there was no Senate floor vote.
   - Source: https://www.scstatehouse.gov/sess126_2025-2026/bills/4767.htm

### A4. Forum-selection clauses (federal overlay)

- ***The Bremen v. Zapata Off-Shore Co.*, 407 U.S. 1 (1972)**: a clause is "prima facie valid" unless shown unreasonable (at 10). It also fails if it contravenes the forum's strong public policy (at 15). https://www.law.cornell.edu/supremecourt/text/407/1 [Verifier: pins confirmed at https://static.case.law/us/407/html/0001-01.html.]
- ***Atlantic Marine Constr. Co. v. U.S. Dist. Ct.*, 571 U.S. 49 (2013)**:
  - § 1404(a) is the tool for enforcing a clause naming another federal forum. The clause gets controlling weight except in exceptional cases.
  - The plaintiff's choice of forum gets no weight.
  - The transfer does not carry the original venue's choice-of-law rules.
  - For a state or foreign forum, the route is forum non conveniens.
  - https://www.law.cornell.edu/supremecourt/text/12-929. [Verifier: U.S. pins confirmed at https://static.case.law/us/571/html/0049-01.html. The § 1404(a) mechanism is at 59; forum non conveniens at 60; controlling weight at 60 and 63; no weight for the plaintiff's choice at 63; no carried-over choice-of-law rules at 64.]
- ***Klaxon Co. v. Stentor Elec. Mfg. Co.*, 313 U.S. 487, 496 (1941)**: a federal diversity court applies the forum state's conflicts rules. https://www.law.cornell.edu/supremecourt/text/313/487
- **Why this matters for the skill:** a forum clause often decides the choice of law in practice. The forum applies its own conflicts rules and its own view of "fundamental policy." Delaware and Massachusetts forums reach different results than California would (see A6 and A7).
- **Race to the courthouse:** *Advanced Bionics Corp. v. Medtronic, Inc.*, 29 Cal.4th 697 (2002), held the trial court erred in restraining Medtronic from pursuing its Minnesota action, on comity and judicial-restraint grounds. [Verifier correction: the court did not decide the § 16600 question. It also held the first-filed rule does not apply between courts of different sovereigns.] https://static.case.law/cal-4th/29/cases/0697-01.json

### A7. Remote workers and multi-state facts

1. ***DraftKings Inc. v. Hermalyn*, No. 24-1443 (1st Cir. Sept. 26, 2024)**:
   - The employee lived in NJ and worked mainly from NJ and NY for a Massachusetts employer, visiting MA "at least 25 times" in 2.5 years. [Verifier correction: the file said "about 25".] He then moved to Los Angeles for a competitor.
   - Applying MA's choice-of-law exception test (from *Oxford*, which tracks § 187(2); the opinion does not name § 187), the court held California lacked a materially greater interest. It distinguished cases where the employee lived and worked in California throughout.
   - It noted § 16600.5's "regardless of where" text, but that did not change the result.
   - The MA-law injunction was affirmed.
   - Source: https://www.ca1.uscourts.gov/sites/ca1/files/opnfiles/24-1443P-01A.pdf. The court's server rejects curl but serves browsers and WebFetch; the text was confirmed from the extracted PDF. The reporter cite is UNVERIFIED.
   - Lesson: **moving to a protective state after leaving may not bring that state's law with you.** The forum's view of where the relationship sat controls.
2. **Statutory location tests differ.** Map the employee's facts to each test separately:
   - CA and MN: reside AND work.
   - MA: reside OR work, 30 days before leaving.
   - CO: forum uses reside OR work; law uses reside AND work, at termination.
   - WA: "Washington-based."
   - DC: more than 50% of work time in DC (§ 32-581.01).
   - FL CHOICE: "primary place of work," which can be a home office.
   - LA: no location test.
3. **Under § 188 cmt. e,** place of performance carries little weight when work is split among states. Domicile and the place of contracting then gain weight. See *Hightower* n.55: a domicile at signing suggested non-competition would be performed there.
4. **Where events happened** matters most for trade-secret tort claims:
   - In Indiana, a contract's choice-of-law clause did not govern the tort claims; they went to lex loci delicti (*Allen*).
   - DTSA claims (18 U.S.C. § 1836) are federal wherever brought: 3-year limit; an injunction may not "prevent a person from entering into an employment relationship." https://www.law.cornell.edu/uscode/text/18/1836
   - § 1838: the DTSA does not preempt state law. https://www.law.cornell.edu/uscode/text/18/1838
   - Indiana UTSA: IC 24-2-3. SC Trade Secrets Act: § 39-8-10 et seq. (https://www.scstatehouse.gov/code/t39c008.php). Under § 39-8-30(D), a trade-secret NDA is not void for lacking time or geography limits, but *Fay* limits that.
   - A case holding that UTSA claims follow Restatement § 145 tort rules is UNVERIFIED.

### A8. Signals that the governing state is uncertain

Flag uncertainty when any of these appear:

1. The clause names a state that is not where the employee lives or works. Most often this is Delaware or the employer's headquarters state.
2. The employee lives in one state and works in another, works remotely for an out-of-state employer, or splits work time across states. The statutory tests in A7 then diverge.
3. The employee lives or works in an override state (CA, CO, LA, MA, MN, WA; or NM, UT, VT for covered professions; or FL, in the employer-favoring direction).
4. The employee moved since signing, or plans to move to a protective state (*DraftKings*).
5. A forum clause names a different state from the choice-of-law clause, or names a forum whose conflicts rules favor enforcement.
6. Litigation is already filed, or likely to be, in another state (race to the courthouse; *Advanced Bionics*).
7. The chosen law would let a court rewrite or reform a covenant the home state would void. Examples: Indiana's eraser rule (*Heraeus*), SC's no-blue-pencil rule (*Poynter* via *Fay*), WI § 103.465, NE.
8. The covenant was signed in a sale-of-business or equity deal. Different rules and exceptions apply (*Hightower*, *Aprio*).
9. The employee is a physician or other healthcare worker. More than a dozen states now have occupation-specific bans (Part B).
10. The contract was signed, renewed or amended near an effective date: WA 2027-06-30, TN 2026-07-01, VA 2025-07-01 and 2026-07-01, WY 2025-07-01, FL 2025-07-01, IN 2025-07-01, ME c.718 (2026). Many new laws apply only to agreements made on or after their effective date.
11. Counsel was involved in signing (the CA § 925(e) exception; *NuVasive*, *Aprio*).
12. The claim is a trade-secret tort, not a contract claim. The clause may not reach it (*Allen*).

### A9. Decision procedure: primary and backup state

A skill could follow these steps.

1. **Collect facts.** Get:
   - employee's state of residence, now and at signing;
   - state(s) where the employee works and the share of time in each;
   - employer HQ state;
   - the contract's choice-of-law and forum clauses (if any);
   - where it was signed and negotiated;
   - signing, renewal and termination dates;
   - occupation (healthcare? broadcast? construction?) and pay;
   - whether the employee had individual counsel;
   - whether a suit is filed or threatened, and where;
   - the claim type (contract covenant, trade secret, or both).
2. **Apply override statutes first.** If the employee meets an override state's location test (A5, A7), that state is the **primary** state for the covenant issue, whatever the clause says. If the employee meets two such tests (for example, lives in MA and works in CO), make one primary and the other backup, and flag the conflict.
3. **Florida check.** If the primary place of work is Florida (or the employer is Florida-headquartered and the clause picks Florida law), and the pay exceeds 2x the annual mean wage of the employer's principal-place-of-business county (the employee's residence county if that place is outside FL), Florida's CHOICE Act may apply regardless of the clause. The act excludes healthcare practitioners. [Verifier: wage-county test corrected; FL-HQ route added from § 542.45(1)(b).] Treat Florida as primary for an employer-mode memo and as a serious backup for an employee-mode memo.
4. **Otherwise, start from the clause.** If a clause exists and the chosen state has a substantial relationship (§ 187(2)(a)), the chosen state is the provisional primary.
5. **Run the § 187(2)(b) test.** Find the default state under § 188/§ 196: usually where the employee mainly works, or where they live if work is split. Then check:
   - Does the default state's law void or narrow the covenant where the chosen law would enforce or reform it?
   - Is that a fundamental policy (a statute, a no-reformation rule, an occupation ban)?
   - If yes, make the **default state primary** and the **chosen state backup**.
   - If no, keep the chosen state primary and the default state backup.
6. **Account for the forum.** If a forum clause or pending suit puts the case in a court that applies its own conflicts rules (Delaware, Massachusetts, a federal court under *Klaxon*), note that the forum's approach may decide steps 4–5. Name the likely forum next to the primary state.
7. **No clause.** The primary state is the § 188 default (place of performance, then domicile). The backup is the state with the next strongest contacts, usually the employer's HQ. [Verifier: a South Carolina forum may instead apply lex loci contractus, the place of signing (*Lister*, A3). When the forum is SC and the signing state differs from the work state, name the signing state as backup and flag it.]
8. **Split claims.** For trade-secret tort claims, add a line naming the state where the misappropriation happened, plus the DTSA. The contract clause may not govern them.
9. **Effective dates.** Check the signing and renewal dates against the Part B change dates before applying a new statute. [Verifier: don't assume every new law is prospective only. Some reach existing agreements or pending proceedings:]
   - WA ESHB 1155 from 2027-06-30, "regardless of when" signed;
   - OR HB 3410, "before, on or after";
   - LA Act 273, which also applies to existing contracts;
   - TN HB 1034, which applies to "proceedings occurring" on/after 2026-07-01.
10. **State the confidence.** Say "uncertain" whenever any A8 signal is present. Name the primary and backup states with one-line reasons, and send them to Grant for confirmation before research starts (see CONTEXT.md, *applied state*).
