Mode: Employee · Applied state: Indiana (why: the letter picks Indiana law, and you live and work in Indiana for an Indiana employer) · Depth: Deep, deep-verified · Reference files current as of 2026-10-20 · Prepared 2026-10-20

## Bottom line
- **Goal:** Uncertain to be reachable: the blank Exhibit A and the any-time, any-subject assignment in §6 put the apps at risk, and no Indiana statute protects off-hours inventions. Fixing §6 before you sign makes it Likely [3][27].
- The non-compete's "own any interest" prong could reach your app business, though Indiana courts void covenants this broad and only strike words, never rewrite them [11][12][13].
- The letter's one case citation, *Hartwell Data Systems, Inc. v. Kessler*, 258 N.E.3d 412 (Ind. 2025), was not found in any source checked [26, NOT FOUND]. Don't rely on it.
- Timing: the Oct. 9 sign-by date has passed and you start Nov. 2. Get a written extension first.

## Path to your goal
1. Before signing: list each app on Exhibit A by name, function, repository, version and date, and state that later versions stay yours (risk #1) [3][7].
2. Before signing: narrow §6 to work made on company time, with company resources, or related to its business, with a signed acknowledgment that the listed apps are yours (risk #2) [2][8].
3. Before signing: strike "or own any interest in" from §3, or carve out the listed apps (risk #3) [11][12].
4. Before signing: register each app's current version with the Copyright Office (risk #1) [4][10].
5. On the job: work on the apps only on your own time, devices and accounts, with no company data, and keep them off any company-managed phone (risks #2, #4) [8][17][22].
6. When leaving: return the laptop intact and copy nothing (risk #6) [18].

## Recommendations
**Before signing**
- (#1) Complete and date Exhibit A as in step 1. Talk to a licensed attorney before you act.
- (#2) Propose this carve-out, a draft for your attorney to review: "Inventions listed on Exhibit A, and later versions developed without Company time, equipment or Confidential Information, remain Employee's sole property." Talk to a licensed attorney before you act.
- (#3) Ask to delete the ownership prong, or limit §3 to your role, your customers and Indiana. Talk to a licensed attorney before you act.
- (#4) Decline BYOD and use a separate phone for company email. Talk to a licensed attorney before you act.
- (#5) Limit §4 to customers and colleagues you actually worked with. Talk to a licensed attorney before you act.
- Ask the employer for a copy of *Hartwell* [26, NOT FOUND]. Talk to a licensed attorney before you act.

**On the job**
- (#2) Never show or use the apps at work. In *Avtec*, the employer's use of a program hurt the employee who wrote it at home [8].

**When leaving**
- (#6) Return the laptop on time. Don't wipe it or copy files from it [17][18].
- (#6) If pay is withheld, Indiana wage law awards fees, and double damages without good faith [20].

## Creative option
**Creative, not standard practice.** Before signing, move the three apps into a company you own by a signed written assignment. §6 can only assign prior inventions you own, and a copyright transfer needs the owner's signature [3].
Why it might work: the letter can't assign what you no longer own [3][5].
**Risk:** Medium: §3's "own any interest" prong would reach your stake in that company, so fix §3 first.
**Enforceability:** Uncertain: no Indiana case tests this against an offer-letter sweep [27].
Run it past a licensed attorney before using it.

**Creative, not standard practice.** Register each app's source code before Nov. 2, using the trade-secret deposit options to keep the code private, and put the registration numbers on Exhibit A.
Why it might work: a registration within five years of publication is prima facie evidence of the facts in the certificate, including authorship [4][10].
**Risk:** Low: code without a human author can't be registered, so separate out any AI-generated code [23].
**Enforceability:** Likely: it is evidence, so the employer's consent isn't needed [4].
Run it past a licensed attorney before using it.

## Adjacent issues flagged
- IP ownership: wholly AI-generated code may carry no copyright [23].
- Whistleblower: §5 lacks the DTSA immunity notice, so the employer can't get DTSA exemplary damages or fees from you [16].
- Also noted: §5 covers all non-public information forever; a federal court applying Indiana law voided a similar NDA [15].
- Wage payment: a laptop charge isn't a listed purpose for an Indiana wage deduction [19].
- Federal non-compete rule: not in effect [24].
- NLRA (deferred): *McLaren Macomb* and *Stericycle* still limit broad confidentiality terms but face challenge [28].
- Unemployment (deferred): a discharge for breach of duty can bar Indiana benefits [29].
- Tax on severance (deferred): severance is FICA wages [30].
- Immigration (deferred): an H-1B worker can't be charged a penalty for leaving early [31].
